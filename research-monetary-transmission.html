Monetary Policy Transmission to Pakistan’s Manufacturing Sector: A Structural Break-Adjusted Vector Error Correction Analysis

Lead author, with Dr. Ayesha Nazuk, NUST · Journal of Policy Modeling (SSCI, Q1)

Abstract

Pakistan’s manufacturing sector faces a fundamental policy dilemma: the State Bank of Pakistan relies on the policy rate as its principal anti-inflation instrument, yet the sector’s acute import dependence makes exchange rate movements a potentially dominant source of output volatility. This paper estimates a structural break-adjusted, six-variable Vector Error Correction Model over 186 monthly observations from January 2006 to June 2021 to rank three transmission channels simultaneously.

Exchange rate shocks explain 27.5% of manufacturing output forecast error variance at a 24-month horizon, against 1.5% attributable to direct interest rate shocks. The long-run exchange rate pass-through elasticity is 6.63, while the interest rate’s long-run effect on prices is much smaller. A pre-2018 subsample reveals a structural reversal: before Pakistan’s IMF programme and currency crisis, the bank credit channel accounted for 50.7% of output variance, while the exchange rate channel contributed only 4.5%. The analysis therefore places exchange-rate exposure at the centre of the industrial transmission story.

Research question

Which channel dominates the transmission of monetary policy to Pakistan’s manufacturing output - the interest rate, the exchange rate, or credit - and does that ranking change once structural breaks in the data are properly accounted for?

Motivation

Pakistan’s manufacturing sector operates under acute dependence on imported inputs, while the central bank leans on the policy rate as its principal instrument. If the interest rate is a weak lever and the exchange rate is the true transmission channel, then relying on rate hikes alone to control inflation imposes collateral costs on industrial output without addressing the actual source of price pressure.

Data

186 monthly observations, January 2006 to June 2021. Six endogenous variables: manufacturing output (QIM), the Wholesale Price Index (WPI), the exchange rate (EXR), the money market rate (MMR), the government bond rate (GB), and credit to the private sector (CMS). Output, prices, the exchange rate, and credit are log-transformed; interest rate variables are retained in percentage-point form.

Methodology

Structural breaks are identified using the Clemente–Montañés–Reyes procedure, including an additive-outlier (AO) break, with controls for the Global Financial Crisis, COVID-19, and seasonality. A Frisch–Waugh–Lovell pre-filtering step residualizes each series against these deterministic controls before the dynamic system is estimated. Structural-break-adjusted unit root tests establish integration order, the Johansen framework identifies two long-run cointegrating relationships, and a Vector Error Correction Model jointly estimates short-run dynamics and long-run adjustment.

Transmission is identified using orthogonalized impulse response functions under a recursive Cholesky ordering (output → prices → exchange rate → money market rate → bond rate → credit), complemented by forecast error variance decomposition. The model passes a diagnostic battery including residual serial correlation, normality, eigenvalue stability, weak exogeneity, and CUSUM parameter stability tests.

Main results

Long-run relationships

Two cointegrating equations are identified, each significant at the 1% level. In the output equation, the exchange rate is the only significant long-run determinant: a 1% depreciation is associated with a 0.956% fall in long-run manufacturing output. The interest rate has no significant direct long-run effect on output.

In the price equation, exchange rate pass-through to prices is 6.63: a 1% depreciation raises long-run wholesale prices by 6.63%. The money market rate carries a positive long-run coefficient of 0.522, consistent with a working-capital cost-push channel, where higher borrowing costs raise producer prices before demand compression can operate. The government bond yield carries a coefficient of −0.527, consistent with a crowding-out effect on private credit.

The 2018 structural shift

A pre-2018 subsample shows a markedly different ranking: the bank credit channel explained 50.7% of output variance, while the exchange rate channel contributed just 4.5%. The transition to a more market-determined exchange rate regime after 2018 reversed this, elevating the exchange rate channel to dominance.

Adjustment and exogeneity

Manufacturing output corrects toward long-run equilibrium at roughly 49% per month. The money market rate itself adjusts to the price equation, consistent with an implicit Taylor rule embedded in the data. Formal tests confirm that wholesale prices, credit, and the bond rate are weakly exogenous, meaning they do not adjust to correct disequilibria; only output and the policy rate do.

Selected evidence

Contribution

  • Ranks three transmission channels - interest rate, exchange rate, and credit - simultaneously within a single structural break-adjusted framework.
  • Identifies a structural regime change around 2018, showing that the dominant transmission channel is not fixed but shifts with the exchange rate regime.
  • Finds no short-run “price puzzle”: prices fall immediately following an interest-rate hike, indicating the model controls for confounds that generate spurious results in comparable studies.
